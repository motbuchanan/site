            How does it know? The math, step by step

            There is no sun service behind this and nothing gets looked up. Your phone works it out from three things: the date, the clock, and where your spot is. Here is the chain, with live numbers for the moment you have picked.

            • Count the days

              It is days since noon on January 1, 2000 (UTC). Astronomers use that moment as a starting line, so everything else is measured from it.

            • Find the sun on its yearly circle

              Seen from Earth, the sun drifts once around the sky each year, about one degree a day. Right now it is around that circle.

            • Apply the tilt

              Earth leans 23.44°. Because of the lean, the sun is straight overhead at latitude at this moment. That number swings from 23.44° north in June to 23.44° south in December. That swing is the seasons, and it is why the sunset slides along the horizon through the year.

            • Spin the Earth

              Earth turns 15° every hour. At your spot the sun is its highest point of the day, which is solar noon.

            • Stand at your spot

              Your latitude () plus those two numbers gives the answer: the sun is the horizon, toward .

              height = asin( sin(lat) * sin(dec) + cos(lat) * cos(dec) * cos(turn) ) bearing = 180 + atan2( sin(turn), cos(turn) * sin(lat) - tan(dec) * cos(lat) ) dec = overhead latitude (the tilt step) turn = angle from the spin step

            • Golden hour is measured by height

              Here, golden hour means the sun is between 4° below and 6° above the horizon. Blue hour is 6° to 4° below. Those cutoffs are a convention among photographers, so treat the edges as soft. To build the day list, the tool checks the sun's height at every minute of the day and notes each time it crosses one of those lines. Sunrise and sunset are counted when the sun's center is 0.83° below the horizon, which is when its top edge shows, because air bends the light a little.

            • Shadows are one more line

              Shadow length is the height of the thing divided by tan(sun height). At 45° a shadow matches the thing that casts it. At 5° it is more than 11 times longer. The shadow always points directly away from the sun.

            • Lining up a shot

              Two taps on the map make a compass direction, from where you stand to where the sun should sit. The tool then walks through the next 365 days. On each day it finds the minute the sun crosses that direction and reads its height at that moment. Days where the height fits what you asked for go in the list. How high the sun appears above the ground at your second tap is the distance to it times tan(sun height).

            • Time zones

              The sun math runs on the exact instant, so it never cares about clocks. Only the labels do. Times are shown in the zone you pick above the clock, and the day runs from midnight to midnight in that zone, even on the two days a year the clocks change.

            What it cannot see: hills, trees, and buildings. It assumes a flat, open horizon, so the real sun can drop behind something earlier than the times say. Compass directions are true north, the way the map is drawn. A phone compass points at magnetic north, which can be several degrees different, so trust the map lines over the needle.

            How Light Scout works

            Tap a spot on the map, pick a day and a time, and it shows where the sun will be from there. Made for planning photos around the light.

            1. The teal dot is where you stand. Tap the map to move it, or search a place.
            2. The yellow sun shows which way the sun is at the time on the time bar. Shadows fall the other way. The curve is its path for the whole day.
            3. Orange on the bar is golden hour, the warm, low light photographers chase. Blue is blue hour, the soft glow before sunrise and after sunset.

            See the light answers "where is the sun at this place and time?" Plan a shot answers "when will the sun sit behind that building, in that gap, over that tree?" You tap where you will stand and where the sun should be, and it lists the days.

            It cannot see hills, trees, or buildings that might block the sun. It works from the date, the clock, and the map.

            Words it uses
            Golden hour
            The stretch after sunrise and before sunset when the sun is low. The light is warm and soft and shadows are long. Here it means the sun is between 4 degrees below and 6 degrees above the horizon, so it can be longer or shorter than an hour.
            Blue hour
            Just before sunrise and just after sunset, when the sun is under the horizon and the sky glows deep blue. Soft light with no shadows. Good for city lights and calm water.
            Sunrise and sunset
            When the top edge of the sun meets a flat horizon. A hill or a building in the way makes the real sunrise later and the real sunset earlier where you stand.
            Backlit and front lit
            Backlit: the sun is behind your subject, facing you. Glowing edges, soft faces. Front lit: the sun is behind you, on your subject. Side light shows texture.
            Shadows
            Always point directly away from the sun. The lower the sun, the longer they are.
            Time zone
            The clock the times are shown in. Light Scout uses your phone's clock unless you change it, which matters when you plan a spot far from home.
            True north
            The top of the map. A phone compass points at magnetic north instead, which can be a few degrees off, so trust the map lines over the needle.
            About this build

            This whole tool is one file.

            I shoot a lot of photos and kept guessing where the sun would be. So I built a map I can tap. The sun math runs on the phone from the date, the clock, and the spot. There is no account and no tracking.

            What leaves your phone: the map pictures come from OpenStreetMap and Esri, and a place search asks OpenStreetMap for matches. Your location, if you share it, is only used on the phone to place the pin.

            Tested against a professional astronomy library: sun direction within 0.06 degrees and sunrise and sunset within a few seconds.

            Map wrong? The street map is drawn by volunteers. You can report a missing building or a wrong road at openstreetmap.org/fixthemap.
