01 / The rule

Why can't you have all three?

The three corners aren't separate dials you can each crank to the top. They pull from one shared pool: the hours in a day, the machines you own, the material on the shelf, the people on shift. Spend more on speed and there's less left for quality and cost. Push it all at one corner and you feel the other two thin out.

02 / The corners

What each corner means on the floor

Good

Quality

Does it meet spec, fit right, and hold up? Measured in defects, rework, and returns.

On the floor: tight tolerances, the right material, careful finishing, real inspection.

Fast

Speed

How quickly it's done and out the door. Lead time, turnaround, on-time delivery.

On the floor: rush jobs, overtime, expedited stock, jumping the queue.

Cheap

Cost

What it takes to make. Material, labor hours, machine time, tooling.

On the floor: standard materials, batched work, no overtime, no waste.
03 / The trade
Good + Fast
You give up cheap

Rush a quality job and you pay for it: overtime, premium material, expedited freight. Same-day and done right means someone stayed late and the good stock got burned.

Good + Cheap
You give up fast

Do it right and do it lean, and it takes time. You wait for the efficient path, batch the run, use standard stock. The well-made, fair-priced part is the one with a lead time.

Fast + Cheap
You give up good

Quick and cheap means corners cut: looser tolerances, skipped steps, whatever material is on hand. It ships tomorrow and it's affordable, but plan on rework.

04 / The balance
Quality sits in the middle, not on a corner.

It's what you get when time, cost, and scope stay balanced. Good shops don't grab a corner and forget it. They decide what each job actually needs, then place the point on purpose.

The demand connection

The customer's pull decides where you sit. When everyone wants it now, speed gets expensive: the price climbs or the quality gives. When there's slack, you can do it right and cheap. The triangle is how a shop prices against whatever the market is pulling for.

05 / The catch

So is it a hard law? Not quite.

Pick-two is true in the moment, with the setup you have today. But two things quietly bend the triangle over time.

1Waste is the loophole

A part sitting in a queue, a wasted walk, a tool nobody can find: that burns time and money at once. Cut it and two corners rise together. Hunting that waste is the whole idea behind lean manufacturing.

2Build it in the right order

You can grow the whole triangle, but not by wishing all three bigger at once. Factory research (Ferdows & De Meyer, 1990) found capability stacks in a sequence, like a cone of sand. As you scroll, watch it fill from the base up.

  • 1QualityMake it right first. Everything else stacks on this.
  • 2DependabilityThen hit the date every time. Predictable delivery.
  • 3SpeedNow you can safely go faster and stay flexible.
  • 4CostLasting low cost comes last, earned on top of the rest.

Cheap is the payoff, not the opening move. Chase cheap first and you get defects and delays that cost more than they saved. Build quality, then reliability, then speed, and low cost tends to follow. On today's setup it's still pick-two, so choose on purpose. Over time, by improving the system itself, you push the whole triangle outward.

Where it comes from
~1800s

A version of "you can't pay little and get a lot" is often pinned on the Victorian critic John Ruskin, likely more legend than record.

~1969

The triangle we draw today traces to Martin Barnes, who mapped cost, time, and quality as three competing points while modeling project cost.

Auto line

Manufacturing runs its own version, QCD: Quality, Cost, Delivery. Sometimes called the "devil's triangle."

1990

Ferdows & De Meyer's sand cone reframed it: capabilities aren't just traded, they're layered, quality at the base, cost as the earned apex.
