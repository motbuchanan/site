How Models Work

What the AI is doing with your words, and how to give it a brief it can use.

what's inside

It writes one piece at a time

Every app in this course was built by me directing an AI model from my phone. I think of it like running a creative department: I give the brief, look at what comes back, and send notes; the model writes the code.

Under the chat window, a language model does one thing over and over. It looks at all the text so far and picks a likely next piece. Then it adds that piece and does it again, until the answer is done. It learned what "likely" means by training on a huge amount of text. Nothing you type while chatting changes what it learned.

That one fact explains most of what surprises people. It writes fluent text even when it's wrong, because fluent is what it was trained to produce. It only knows what you put in front of it plus what it learned in training. And a vague request gets a guess, because the model usually fills every gap with the most ordinary choice.

Why it's confident when it's wrong

The toy picks words that tend to follow each other. It has no idea if the sentence is true. Real models are vastly bigger and much better, but they're still picking likely text, so any fact you plan to rely on gets checked.

tokens

It reads in chunks called tokens

Before the model sees your message, it gets cut into tokens: common words stay whole, long or rare words get split into pieces. Limits and prices are counted in tokens. For English, a rough rule is about four characters per token. Type something below.

This splitter is a rough picture. Real tokenizers learn their pieces from data, and each model family cuts text a little differently.

the desk

It only sees what's on the desk

The model's context window is like a desk with room for so many pages. Everything in the chat has to fit on it: your messages, its replies, any files. When this toy desk fills up, the oldest page slides off, and the model can't see it anymore.

Why I write handoff docs

A new chat starts with an empty desk. When a big build continues tomorrow, I end today with a handoff document: what's built, what's decided, what's next. The next chat reads it first. Memory features work the same way: saved notes get put back on the desk at the start.

the brief

Every gap gets filled with the most ordinary choice

"Make me a website" gets the same safe website anyone would get. Each thing you add to a brief takes one guess away from the model. Switch ingredients on and watch what it would have to guess shrink.

My favorite ingredient

Many of my briefs end with "ask me questions first, one at a time." The model narrows things down with me before it builds, and the first version comes back much closer to what I had in mind.
