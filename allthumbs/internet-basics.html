The Second After You Tap

What the internet actually is, and where your request goes.

The internet is not a cloud and not magic. It is millions of computers passing little envelopes to their neighbors, very fast. Once you can picture the envelope's trip, half the error messages you have ever seen start making sense.

You already live with this

part 1 · the three roles

Ask, answer, pass along

Strip away the jargon and there are only three jobs on the internet.

Client: asks
Your phone, your laptop, your TV. A client sends requests: "give me this page." That is the whole job.
tap
Server: answers
Just a computer that answers requests. Not a special machine, not a vault. A computer, usually in a big room with thousands of others, running a program that replies.
tap
Router: passes along
Routers move envelopes one hop closer. No router knows the whole internet. Each one only knows its neighbors, and that turns out to be enough.

part 2 · the address book

Names are for you, numbers are for machines

Machines find each other by number. Nobody wants to memorize numbers, so the internet keeps a giant shared address book called DNS: type a name, get back the number. This one is real and working:

Claim your own name

When a real lookup fails you get NXDOMAIN: "that name is not in the book." You have probably seen it in an error message without knowing it was just a failed address-book check.

part 3 · the trip

Break the internet (it reroutes)

This is a real routable network. Send a request and a genuine pathfinder computes the route hop by hop. Then tap routers to knock them out and send again. The reroute you will see is not scripted: the path is recomputed each time from whatever is still alive.

Tap Send request to mail an envelope from your phone to the server.

This is the internet's founding trick: no single computer is in charge, so losing machines does not lose the network. Notice what happens if you knock out the one router your home connects through, though.

part 4 · the envelope's contents

Requests, responses, and 404

What is actually written in the envelope? Barely anything. A request is mostly one line: an action and a path, like GET /menu. The answer comes back with a status code. This mini server is real: it has exactly four pages and it computes every response.

200 OK
Everything worked. You get this hundreds of times a day and never see it, because good news is invisible.
tap
404 Not Found
The connection worked. The server answered. The answer is: no such page. A 404 is a successful conversation about a missing thing.
tap
301 Moved
"That page lives at a new address now." Your browser follows the forwarding note automatically, usually before you notice.
tap
500 Server Error
The server answered, but its own program crashed while building the reply. Not your fault, not your device. Their kitchen caught fire.
tap
part 5 · at your house

The last few feet

What your router actually is
Your house's post office: a small computer that passes envelopes between your devices and the wider network. Everything on your wifi goes through it.
tap
Why restarting the router works
It is a small computer, and restarting wipes its memory clean, exactly like the previous explainer in this course. A confused post office gets a fresh start.
tap
Wifi vs cell data
Two different first hops to the same internet. Wifi hands your envelope to your router; cell data hands it to a tower. After that, same roads.
tap
Why video calls stutter
Envelopes take turns on busy roads. When too many pile up at one hop, some arrive late, and late video is stutter. Congestion, not distance.
tap
part 6 · the honest verdict

What to keep, what to skip

Keep this

Request and response is the whole conversation. A server is just a computer that answers. DNS turns names into numbers. Envelopes hop router to router, each knowing only its neighbors, and the network reroutes around damage. Status codes are the server talking to you: 404 means it answered.

Skip for now

TCP handshakes, ports, IP address math, the protocol zoo. All real machinery under the model above, none of it needed to reason clearly about "why is this not loading."
