Why I made it

My son and I were learning chess together, and I was already building a chess game for the family Game Shelf. I wanted one app that teaches the game and lets you play it, with no gap between the lesson and the board, so we could get at the deeper strategy together. The lessons run on the same board you play on.

How it came together

It is a trainer-first fork of my chess game. The first engine was easy to beat, somewhere around a 500 or 600 rating. Over several rounds of updates, and a lot of reading about how other chess engines think, it learned to look harder and further ahead. Level 10 now plays at roughly a 1500 level by a rough comparison against online rating scales. Lessons are six tiers, from the pieces up through tricky rules, first skills, seeing the board, winning won games, and a brush-up. Tier 1 is complete and the rest are planned. The computer plays Black at ten levels, with a coach that warns you before a blunder and a hint button.

What I learned

Keeping the lesson and the game on the same board matters more than any amount of explaining text. A coach that flags the mistake after the fact teaches better than one that nags before every move. And an engine gets stronger in steps, not all at once: every rating jump came from one specific idea about how to search.
