13 moves

Each episode is named for a real opening or chess idea, and its story lives out what that idea does on the board. A player reads the title and knows what's coming; everyone else gets it by the credits.

Two alternates if the story wants them. The Bongcloud is a joke opening where you walk your king out on move two (good for a comedy episode about winning while showing off). Stalemate means nobody has a legal move, so nobody wins.

The order follows the story, whatever it turns out to be.

The hidden board

Every episode secretly plays the opening it's named for, one move at a time, hidden in the set dressing. Nobody says it out loud. It's there for whoever hits pause. Here's how The Scotch Game could play out across one episode. Tap a frame and the board follows.

Start position

The rules stay simple so a viewer can learn them. White's moves go on white signs and Black's go on black ones; squares are written as text (door numbers, plates, routes) while pieces show up as images, so a horse logo is a knight. A capture gets an X, like tape or a crossed-out sign. Every move has to read on a single freeze frame, and they always land in order.

More shots

A few other ways the board can live inside a frame.

Two more ideas that don't need a drawing. Each episode's edit can match the speed of the game inside it, so a blitz episode cuts fast and tight while a slow game gets room to breathe (with a chess-clock tap as the transition sound). And players will freeze-frame every board on screen, so a chess consultant checking positions keeps the show honest with the exact audience it's built to reward.

One game, 13 episodes

Under the episode openings, a second hidden layer plays a single famous game across the whole season, a few moves per episode. The last image of the finale is the final move. Any of these three would carry a season, and each one says something different.

17moves

The Opera Game

Paul Morphy vs. two noblemen · Paris, 1858

Two rich men team up against one player in their private box at the opera. He keeps giving up material to get his pieces working, then finishes it fast.

23moves

The Immortal Game

Adolf Anderssen vs. Lionel Kieseritzky · London, 1851

Anderssen sacrifices a bishop, both rooks and his queen; then he mates with the small pieces he has left.

44moves

Kasparov's Immortal

Garry Kasparov vs. Veselin Topalov · Wijk aan Zee, 1999

Kasparov gives up a rook and chases the enemy king out of its corner, across the board and deep into his own side. It starts slow and turns into a long chase.
